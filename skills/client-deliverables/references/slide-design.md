# Slide design for consulting work

Use this reference when making or redesigning slides. The aim is a convincing visual argument that an executive can read, discuss, and edit. Follow the supplied template and visual direction first. A user's slide count, reading conditions, and mandatory content govern the result; there is no default deck length or required mix of layouts.

## Choose a visual direction

Before building, decide how the content should feel and read: a precise financial review, an editorial strategy narrative, a field-based operating diagnosis, or another direction supported by the brief. Translate that into typography, color, spacing, chart treatment, and imagery. Keep this as a working design decision, not another client-facing framework.

Use a coherent grid and a deliberate type hierarchy. The takeaway, evidence, annotation, and source should have distinct visual roles. Give the main exhibit space to explain itself. Use whitespace to separate ideas and emphasize relationships. Choose available fonts that render reliably and respect the client's typography.

Keep color meanings stable across the deck. Reserve emphasis for the recommended option, material change, or decision threshold. Numeric increases and decreases are not inherently good or bad; a falling cost and a falling profit need different business interpretations. Contrast can come from scale, weight, position, and space as well as color.

## Compose around the argument

Sketch the slide's purpose, central evidence, and composition before coding. Choose a form because it clarifies that evidence. Useful approaches include:

| What the slide needs to accomplish | Possible composition | What makes it work |
|---|---|---|
| Establish a consequential finding | An oversized supported number or short conclusion paired with its defining exhibit | The number has a denominator, period, and comparator. It leads into the argument without becoming an unsupported slogan. |
| Explain a change | A wide bridge or trend with annotations at the drivers | The audience can see what changed and where; the explanation sits beside the relevant marks. |
| Show differences across segments | Aligned small multiples, ranked bars, or a dot plot | Common scales and definitions reveal patterns without a large prose table. Direct labels reduce legend hunting. |
| Compare strategic options | A flat comparison with a visually emphasized recommendation and explicit trade-offs | Columns share the same criteria. Feasibility and uncertainty stay visible; the recommendation is not just the most colorful box. |
| Explain how work breaks down | An editable process or operating-model diagram, with the constrained handoff emphasized | Positions and connections carry meaning. Add only the roles, queues, or interfaces needed to explain the issue. |
| Connect evidence to a proposed response | An asymmetric evidence-and-implication composition | Evidence receives the space it needs; a short adjacent interpretation explains the business consequence. |
| Make execution tangible | A time-scaled roadmap or dependency map | Duration, sequence, ownership, and decision points are legible without a wall of activity labels. |
| Establish setting or customer experience | A purposeful photograph, sourced visual, or clearly labeled illustration integrated with sparse text | The asset conveys relevant context and is cropped intentionally. It does not pretend to be evidence of an unobserved event. |
| Request a decision | A precise ask with the economics, conditions, or trade-off the decision-maker needs | The reader can tell what is being authorized. Avoid a generic “Next steps” page when a concrete choice is available. |

Several exhibits can support the same point. Conversely, a single complex exhibit may deserve a whole slide. Preserve a plain setup title when the slide establishes definitions or context; use a supported takeaway when the slide demonstrates a finding. Do not force every title into an imperative, a question, or a slogan.

## Create variation with continuity

Let the sequence alternate naturally between orientation, evidence, comparison, and decision. A dense analytical page can be followed by a simpler implication when the story warrants it. Vary the composition and visual scale while keeping fonts, alignment, and color meaning consistent. Repeated layouts are useful for direct comparisons or a required template; repetition without that purpose makes distinct ideas look interchangeable.

Avoid defaulting to a title above equal-sized cards, generic icons, and short bullet lists on every page. Also avoid repeated badges, decorative section labels, dashboard panels, and oversized footers that compete with the evidence. Use flat compositions and native analytical objects where they suit the argument. Visual interest should come from the subject, relationships, typography, and emphasis.

A cover, divider, summary, agenda, or closing page must serve the requested use. A short update may need none of them. Do not add pages or decorative assets merely to create variety, and do not force a photo into an analytical slide that works better as a chart.

## Make exhibits carry the reasoning

Reduce nonessential axes, gridlines, borders, and legends while preserving scale and interpretation. Place labels near the values they identify. Annotate the inflection, variance, outlier, or threshold that supports the title; avoid repeating the title in a separate callout. Keep full data and calculations available when an exhibit shows a selected view.

Order categories deliberately, align comparable quantities, and use consistent precision. Distinguish observed values, forecasts, scenarios, and targets through explicit labels and suitable visual treatments. Preserve material caveats beside the affected claim, but do not repeat the same caveat across every page unless each page would otherwise mislead when read independently.

Use actual client assets when supplied. Source external images through permitted tools and record attribution or usage requirements. Label generated or illustrative visuals where they could be mistaken for real people, facilities, customer research, or observed results. Keep tables, charts, and required diagrams editable when the output format and tools permit.

## Review the whole experience

Inspect a contact sheet or slide sequence to assess visual rhythm and storyline. Check whether the most consequential point receives the most emphasis, whether adjacent pages are unnecessarily repetitive, and whether the deck feels like one designed document. Variation by itself is not success: the sequence must become easier to follow.

Inspect each rendered slide at the intended reading size. Check line breaks, typography, alignment, crowded labels, image crops, chart annotations, contrast, source readability, and claim-exhibit agreement. Compare against a supplied template when relevant. A valid file and an absence of overlaps establish only part of the quality bar. Revise weak compositions as well as technical defects, then recheck the affected slides and the sequence.
